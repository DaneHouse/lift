# Rehab content: core, upper arm, elbow, wrist and hand

Regions: `core`, `upper-arm`, `elbow`, `wrist-hand`. Written fresh for LIFT (clean-room). LIFT never diagnoses: the "causes" lists are things that commonly cause pain in each area, not statements about the user.

Research note: most publisher pages (NHS, AAOS, Cleveland Clinic, PubMed, BJSM full text, JOSPT PDF) were blocked from direct fetch in this environment. Claims below rest on search-result summaries of those pages plus a few secondary pages. Stage durations are mostly "general practice" and are aligned to the app's agreed minimums (Protect 3, Restore 10, Rebuild 14, Return 14 days). Anything not verified is marked "general practice, unverified". Exercise sets, reps and cues are general practice unless a source is named.

General loading principle used for all four regions (from tendon-loading and abdominal-strain sources, see Sources): start with pain-limited holds, then slow light load, then heavier slow load, then speed and sport-specific work. A pain level up to about 3 of 10 during an exercise that settles by the next morning is a commonly used guide (general practice, unverified for LIFT).

## core — Abs / core

### Common causes of pain here

- A sudden pull in the abdominal wall during a twist, throw, or a hard rep with a brace that gave out (sudden).
- Heavy sit-ups, hanging leg raises or ab-wheel work added too quickly (gradual overuse).
- Repeated rotation under load, such as woodchops or one-sided carries (gradual overuse).
- Sharp pain with coughing, sneezing or laughing after a hard session (can follow a strain; see red flags if a bulge appears).
- Rib-edge or side pain after a twisting or side-bending movement, from the oblique muscles (sudden or gradual).
- Pain in the lower abdomen or groin that comes with straining; this can have causes other than a muscle and is covered under red flags (gradual or sudden).
- Soreness from a big jump in training volume (gradual, usually settles in days).

### Aggravating movements

- `flex-trunk`: crunches, sit-ups and leg raises shorten and load the abdominal wall directly.
- `rotate`: twisting loads the obliques, often the first thing to flare.
- `extend-trunk`: arching stretches a sore abdominal wall.
- `press-over`: heavy overhead lifts need a hard brace and an arch.
- `hinge`: heavy bracing and straining under load (deadlifts) raise abdominal pressure.

### Stage-by-stage rehab

**Protect.** Goal: calm the area and keep normal daily movement without pain. Min time in stage: 3 days (general practice; one tennis federation guide describes building up after roughly 1 to 5 days once the worst pain settles, see ITF source).

### Progression criteria notes

- Move up when: min days met, last 3 check-ins at 4 or 5, at least 75 percent of rehab done, and (region-specific) no pain with cough, sneeze or deep breath, and the current exercises feel easy to hold at the next day.
- Rotation and flexion loading should only start after isometric holds are pain-free (ITF, PBATS sources).
- Drop back when: any check-in of 2 or lower, 2 skipped sessions, or pain on coughing or sneezing returns.
- A hard-to-explain bulge or a pain that stays at rest is a red flag, not a reason to just drop a stage.

### Red flags specific to this region

Beyond the general five.

- A bulge in the abdomen or groin that shows with straining, coughing or standing (possible hernia). Stop loading the area and see a doctor.
- Severe or sudden abdominal pain, repeated vomiting, a bulge that goes firm, tender or cannot be pushed back, or skin over it turning red or purple. Go to emergency care now (NHS Wales hernia page via search summary).
- Blood in urine, fever, or pain with a hard, swollen belly. Seek urgent care.
- Pain after a direct blow to the belly that is getting worse. Seek urgent care.

## upper-arm — Biceps / triceps

### Common causes of pain here

- A heavy or awkward lift with the elbow bent, sometimes with a pop felt in front of the elbow or in the upper arm (sudden; see red flags).
- Sudden pain at the back of the elbow when pressing or catching a fall (sudden).
- Ache where the biceps tendon meets the elbow after high-volume curls and chin-ups (gradual overuse).
- Deep ache at the back of the elbow after heavy bench, dips, pushdowns or overhead extensions (gradual overuse; triceps tendon irritation is uncommon but reported in lifters, per practitioner source).
- Muscle strain mid-belly after a hard set, with tenderness and bruising (sudden).
- Soreness after new exercises or a jump in volume (gradual, short-lived).
- Higher risk reported with certain medications or steroid use, and prior tendon problems (triceps rupture sources, secondary).

### Aggravating movements

- `curl`: loads the biceps and its tendon, especially at the bottom.
- `extend`: pushdowns and extensions load the triceps and its tendon at the elbow.
- `press-flat`: bench and dips load the triceps hard at lockout.
- `press-over`: overhead pressing loads the triceps through full range.
- `pull-vert`: pull-ups and pulldowns load the biceps, especially underhand.
- `pull-horiz`: rows load the biceps and often end in a heavy grip.

### Stage-by-stage rehab

**Protect.** Goal: calm the arm and keep it moving. Min time: 3 days (general practice).

### Progression criteria notes

- Move up when: min days met, last 3 check-ins at 4 or 5, at least 75 percent of rehab done, and the arm is no more sore the next morning after loaded sets.
- Go heavier only when the lighter version has been pain-free for at least a couple of sessions (general practice, unverified).
- Drop back when: any check-in of 2 or lower, 2 skipped sessions, or new pain at the elbow tendon after loading.
- Note that a tendon problem can look like a slow plateau: if each stage is stalling, a physio visit is more useful than another cycle.

### Red flags specific to this region

- A pop or snap at the front of the elbow or upper arm, followed by bruising, a new lump or bunching of the muscle, or sudden weakness turning the forearm palm up (possible biceps tendon tear). Stop loading and see a doctor promptly; early assessment matters (Cleveland Clinic, eorthopod via search summary).
- Sudden pain at the back of the elbow with swelling or bruising, and trouble straightening the arm against gravity (possible triceps tendon tear). Stop loading and get assessed soon (occ-ortho, handsurgeonsnyc via search summary).
- A deformed-looking elbow or arm after a fall or heavy lift. Urgent care or emergency.

## elbow — Elbow (outer, inner, back)

### Common causes of pain here

- Outer elbow: repeated gripping and wrist-extending work, such as heavy pulls, reverse curls or a new racquet or tool (gradual overuse; commonly called tennis elbow, JOSPT notes it often recurs).
- Inner elbow: repeated wrist-flexing and gripping, heavy curls, rows and throwing (gradual overuse; commonly called golfer's elbow).
- Back of elbow: heavy triceps or pressing volume, or repeated hard lockouts (gradual overuse).
- A sudden twinge during a heavy pull or press (sudden).
- A fall onto the arm or a direct knock (sudden; see red flags).
- Nerve irritation at the inner elbow from leaning on it or prolonged bending, with tingling in the little finger (gradual; this is a red flag item).
- Swelling over the point of the elbow from leaning or a knock (gradual or sudden).
- Rapid jumps in grip-heavy volume such as pull-ups, deadlifts with straps off, or climbing (gradual).

### Aggravating movements

- `grip`: gripping drives the wrist muscles that attach at the elbow.
- `curl`: curls load both the inner elbow and the forearm muscles.
- `extend`: loading the triceps stresses the back of the elbow.
- `press-flat`: lockout under load can pinch or tug at the elbow.
- `pull-vert`: hanging and pulling load the forearm and elbow flexors.
- `pull-horiz`: heavy rows with a hard grip.

### Stage-by-stage rehab

**Protect.** Goal: settle the tendon and keep pain-free movement. Min time: 3 days (general practice).

### Progression criteria notes

- Move up when: min days met, last 3 check-ins at 4 or 5, at least 75 percent of rehab done, and grip tasks (carrying shopping, jar lids) feel normal.
- Check pain after training: a rise that is gone by the next morning is a commonly used pass (general practice, unverified).
- Evidence for eccentric loading in inner-elbow problems is rated low certainty (2026 review via search summary), so the app should not claim a specific protocol works.
- Drop back when: any check-in of 2 or lower, 2 skipped sessions, or pain returns with ordinary grip tasks.

### Red flags specific to this region

- An elbow that looks out of shape, cannot straighten or bend, or feels locked after a fall or knock. Go to emergency or urgent care with X-ray. Do not force it (Bon Secours, NHS Greater Glasgow search summaries).
- Numbness, tingling or weakness in the ring or little finger, or a cold, pale or discoloured hand after elbow injury. Emergency care if sudden or after a fall; otherwise see a doctor soon.
- A pop with bruising at the front or back of the elbow. See "upper-arm" red flags.
- Redness, heat and swelling at the point of the elbow with fever (possible infection of the bursa). Urgent care.

## wrist-hand — Forearm / wrist / hand / fingers

### Common causes of pain here

- Pain on the thumb side of the wrist with gripping or twisting, often after heavy pulls or new grip-heavy volume (gradual overuse; commonly called de Quervain's irritation, linked to weightlifters, climbers and rowers in secondary sources).
- Pain on the little-finger side of the wrist during pressing or racquet-type loads (gradual overuse).
- Front-rack or push-up wrist pain from loaded wrist extension (gradual overuse).
- A fall onto the outstretched hand (sudden; see red flags).
- Forearm muscle tightness or ache from a jump in grip or curl volume (gradual).
- Finger pain from loaded gripping, such as pull-ups, rope climbs or a barbell turning in the hand (sudden or gradual).
- Tingling in the thumb and first fingers, often at night or when holding a bar (gradual; this is a red flag item).
- A catch or click in the wrist after a twist (sudden or gradual).

### Aggravating movements

- `grip`: gripping loads forearm muscles and the small joints of the hand.
- `press-flat`: push-ups and bench press load the wrist in extension.
- `press-over`: overhead lockouts and front-rack positions bend the wrist back.
- `pull-vert`: hanging puts the whole body on the grip.
- `pull-horiz`: heavy rows end in a hard grip.
- `curl`: wrist position under heavy curls can irritate the forearm and wrist.

### Progression criteria notes

- Move up when: min days met, last 3 check-ins at 4 or 5, at least 75 percent of rehab done, and everyday tasks (opening jars, carrying bags, push-up on forearms) are pain-free.
- Wrist loading should be pain-free in neutral before adding bent-wrist positions (general practice, unverified).
- Drop back when: any check-in of 2 or lower, 2 skipped sessions, or catching, clicking or night pain returns.
- Persistent pain beyond several weeks, swelling, catching or weakness is a reason to see a hand therapist or sports physician (review summaries).

### Red flags specific to this region

- Pain after a fall onto the hand with swelling or tenderness at the base of the thumb (the small dip near the thumb). Stop loading and be seen within about a day; an early X-ray can miss a scaphoid fracture (orthoinfo scaphoid via search summary).
- Numbness or tingling in the fingers, or a hand or fingers that look white, blue or feel cold. Emergency care if sudden or after injury; otherwise see a doctor soon.
- A visibly bent or deformed wrist or finger, or a finger that cannot straighten. Urgent care.
- Hot, red, swollen finger or hand with fever. Urgent care.
