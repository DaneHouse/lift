# Rehab content: hip, groin, hamstring, low back

> Research draft for Kassra's review. Not medical advice. Content is written for LIFT from the cited sources and has not been reviewed by a clinician.

Notes on method and limits:

- Written fresh in our own words. Nothing copied from sources.
- Network access blocked nhs.uk, jospt.org and pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov for direct fetching. Claims below that rest on those sites come from search-result excerpts of the pages, not full-text reads. Those lines are marked "(excerpt only)". Please have a clinician check the final menus.
- Stage minimum days (Protect 3, Restore 10, Rebuild 14, Return 14) are the agreed constants in INJURIES_SPEC.md. They are "general practice" unless a source is named. Where the evidence suggests slower recovery, a note says so.
- Exercise doses (sets, reps, seconds) are "general practice, unverified" throughout. No source gave a fixed dose for a gym app audience.
- LIFT never diagnoses. Conditions are listed only as things that commonly cause pain in the area.
- Item format: sets x reps, or sets x seconds for time-mode items. "per side" is implied for single-limb items.

## hip — Hip / glute

### Common causes of pain here

- Gradual overuse: irritated tendons on the outer hip (often called gluteal tendinopathy or trochanteric pain). Tender on the bony point of the outer hip, worse lying on that side, crossing legs, or standing on one leg. Evidence: LEAP trial protocol, physiotutors.
- Gradual: bursa irritation on the outer hip, with similar symptoms to the above and often not separable from it by the person.
- Gradual or after a twist: pinching at the front of the hip when bending deep (hip impingement shape). Deep front or groin pain, worse in deep squats and sitting low.
- Sudden or gradual: damage to the rim cartilage of the hip socket (labrum). May click, catch, or feel like it locks. Often follows a twist or a long run of impingement-type loading.
- Gradual: general hip joint wear (osteoarthritis). Stiff in the morning, aching after activity, more common over 40.
- Sudden: glute or hip flexor muscle strain from a sprint, kick, or heavy lift.
- Gradual: referred pain from the low back or sacroiliac area, felt in the buttock or hip.
- Sudden after a fall or impact: bone injury, including fracture. This is a red flag, see below.

### Aggravating movements

| Tag        | Why                                                                                                   |
| ---------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| squat      | Deep hip bend can pinch the front of the hip and loads the outer hip tendons.                         |
| hinge      | Heavy hip hinge loads the glutes and hamstrings at their attachments and can irritate a sore buttock. |
| lunge      | Single-leg stance drops the pelvis and compresses the outer hip tendons.                              |
| jump-run   | Repeated impact and single-leg landings load the hip joint and tendons.                               |
| hip-thrust | Strong glute contraction at end range can flare an irritated glute tendon early on.                   |
| kneel-deep | Deep hip flexion and rotation under load can pinch the front of the hip.                              |
| rotate     | Twisting on a planted foot stresses the joint rim and the capsule.                                    |

**Protect.** Goal: calm the hip down and keep it moving without pinching or compressing it.
Typical minimum time: 3 days (general practice).

| hinge       | 0196 Cable pull through (with rope) | Lighter hinge load and easy to stop where it hurts.        |
| lunge       | 0413 Dumbbell squat                 | Two feet on the floor, no pelvis drop on one side.         |
| hip-thrust  | 3013 Low glute bridge on floor      | Smaller range and load.                                    |
| jump-run    | 0003 Air bike                       | Keeps the legs working with no impact.                     |

### Progression criteria notes

- Move up when: minimum days passed, last 3 check-ins at 4 or 5, at least 75% of rehab done in the last 2 weeks.
- Also look for: no pain at night or lying on the side, no pinch deep in the hip on the current stage's exercises, and next-day soreness no higher than before.
- Drop back when: any check-in of 2 or lower, or 2 skipped rehab sessions in a row (the agreed rule). Also drop back if the hip starts to catch, click painfully, or lock.
- Position advice that is commonly given for outer hip tendon pain (general practice, not trial-proven): avoid crossing legs, avoid standing with weight hung on one hip, put a pillow between the knees when side-lying.

### Red flags specific to this region

- Cannot put weight on the leg, or severe sudden hip pain, especially after a fall or impact, or the leg looks shorter or turned out. Action: stop loading and seek emergency care.
- Hip or groin pain with fever, or a hot, red, swollen joint. Action: see a doctor the same day.
- Hip pain that keeps getting worse at rest or at night. Action: stop loading the area and see a physio or doctor.
- Locking or catching that stops full movement. Action: stop loading the area and see a physio or doctor.
- Hip pain in a young person that changes how they walk. Action: see a doctor promptly.

## groin — Groin / inner thigh

### Common causes of pain here

- Sudden: adductor muscle strain, from a sprint, kick, change of direction, or a wide stance slip. Pain on the inner thigh, often with a felt pull. (physiotutors, Serner cohort)
- Gradual overuse: adductor tendon irritation near the pubic bone, common with lots of side-to-side movement and running.
- Gradual: pain at the front of the hip that feels like groin pain (hip joint problems such as impingement or labral irritation). Worse in deep flexion.
- Gradual: hip flexor (iliopsoas) irritation, felt at the front of the hip and high groin, worse lifting the knee.
- Gradual: pain over the pubic bone, sometimes with sit-up and kick movements.
- Gradual: pain in the lower abdomen or groin crease with coughing or hard effort (abdominal wall area). A doctor is needed to tell this from a hernia.
- Referred pain from the low back, hip, or from something inside the pelvis or abdomen.

The 2015 Doha consensus groups athletic groin pain into adductor-related, iliopsoas-related, inguinal-related, pubic-related and hip-related. LIFT uses this only as background; it does not sort the user into a group. (physiotutors excerpt)

### Aggravating movements

| Tag        | Why                                                                        |
| ---------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| squat      | Wide and deep squats stretch and load the adductors.                       |
| hinge      | Sumo-style and wide-stance deadlifts load the inner thigh heavily.         |
| lunge      | Lateral and long-step lunges stretch the adductors under load.             |
| jump-run   | Sprinting, cutting and kicking are the usual triggers for adductor strain. |
| flex-trunk | Sit-ups and hanging leg raises load the lower abdominal and groin area.    |
| rotate     | Fast twisting on one planted leg stresses the groin attachments.           |

### Stage-by-stage rehab

Typical minimum time: 3 days (general practice).

### Progression criteria notes

- Move up when: minimum days passed, last 3 check-ins at 4 or 5, at least 75% of rehab done in 2 weeks.
- Clinic guidance (physiotutors excerpt, illustrative only) used pain-free daily life, 30 minutes of easy cardio with no pain, and a 30 second short-lever Copenhagen hold with pain under 3 out of 10 before the last phase. Treat these as examples, not proven thresholds.
- A delphi report said adductor strength alone does not show readiness to return to sport (excerpt only). So Return should also include a pain-free, hard-effort run or side-step test.
- Drop back when: any check-in of 2 or lower, or 2 skipped rehab sessions in a row. Also drop back if squeezing the knees together hurts more than at the start of the stage.

### Red flags specific to this region

- Bulge in the groin crease or scrotum, or pain that spikes with coughing. Action: see a doctor (could be a hernia).
- Testicular pain or swelling. Action: urgent medical care.
- Groin pain after a fall or impact, or cannot bear weight. Action: emergency care.
- Groin pain with fever, or a hot swollen joint. Action: same-day doctor.
- Pain that is deep in the hip with locking or catching. Action: stop loading and see a physio or doctor.

## hamstring — Back of thigh

### Common causes of pain here

- Sudden: hamstring muscle strain, usually during sprinting, kicking or a fast stretch (for example a split or a heavy RDL going wrong). Often a felt pull or sharp pain mid thigh. Reinjury is common, about one in three within a year in the review cited. (Heiderscheit 2010 excerpt)
- Gradual overuse: tendon pain right under the buttock at the sit bone (proximal hamstring tendinopathy). Hurts sitting, long strides, hills and deep hinges. Can take months to settle. (PHT review and commentary)
- Sudden: a larger tear or pull at the sit bone attachment. May involve bruising and a popping feeling.
- Gradual: tightness and pain referred from the low back or sciatic nerve (burning, tingling or pins and needles down the back of the leg).
- Gradual: sitting pressure on the sit bone from long hours in a hard seat.
- Gradual: an overloaded hamstring because of weak glutes or poor pelvis control during running.

### Aggravating movements

| Tag          | Why                                                                          |
| ------------ | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| hinge        | Deadlifts, RDLs and good mornings lengthen and load the hamstrings the most. |
| jump-run     | Sprinting is the usual cause of strains and the main reinjury trigger.       |
| lunge        | Long steps put the hamstring on stretch while it works.                      |
| squat        | Deep squats and low-bar squats load the sit bone attachment at the bottom.   |
| hip-thrust   | Strong hip extension at end range can irritate a sore hamstring early on.    |
| extend-trunk | Back extensions and reverse hypers pull on the hamstring origin.             |

### Stage-by-stage rehab

**Protect.** Goal: let the injury settle and keep the muscle quietly active.
Typical minimum time: 3 days (general practice). Days to pain-free walking is one predictor of return time (JOSPT 2022 excerpt).

### Progression criteria notes

- Move up when: minimum days passed, last 3 check-ins 4 or 5, at least 75% of rehab done.
- Look for: walking, stairs and the current stage's exercises are pain free with the leg straight, and no stiffness next morning.
- Before running at speed: the leg should handle a hard hinge and a bridge with no pain, and a gradual run build-up should come first (general practice).
- Drop back when: any check-in of 2 or lower, 2 skipped sessions in a row, or a sharp pull during a rep.

### Red flags specific to this region

- A "pop" or tearing feeling with bruising down the back of the thigh. Action: stop loading and see a physio or doctor soon (a sit bone tear can need a scan).
- Cannot walk normally or cannot put weight through the leg after a sudden injury. Action: urgent care.
- Pain, swelling and warmth in the calf or thigh with no clear cause. Action: urgent medical care, could be a blood clot.
- Burning, tingling or numbness down the back of the leg. Action: stop loading and see a physio or doctor.

## low-back — Low back

### Common causes of pain here

- Sudden: muscle or ligament strain from lifting, twisting or a bad rep. Most common cause, and it usually improves over weeks. (NHS excerpt)
- Gradual overuse: stiffness and ache from repeated bending, long sitting, or too much training volume.
- Sudden or gradual: irritation of a spinal joint or disc. May cause pain on one side, sometimes into the buttock or leg.
- Gradual or sudden: nerve root irritation (sciatica-type pain). Pain, tingling or numbness down the leg below the knee.
- Gradual: stress on the lower spine from repeated arching, common in athletes who extend a lot.
- Gradual: general age-related wear and stiffness.
- Gradual: pain linked with stress, sleep or low activity. The guideline view is that most persistent pain is helped by movement and exercise, not rest. (JOSPT 2021 excerpt)
- Sudden after a fall or heavy impact: bone injury. A red flag, see below.

### Aggravating movements

| Tag          | Why                                                                                       |
| ------------ | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| hinge        | Deadlifts, good mornings and bent rows load the lower spine directly.                     |
| squat        | Heavy back squats load the spine; losing a neutral spine at the bottom adds strain.       |
| extend-trunk | Back extensions and arching hit an irritable back, especially from a spinal joint source. |
| flex-trunk   | Sit-ups and crunches repeatedly bend the spine, which can irritate a disc-type back.      |
| rotate       | Twisting under load adds shear to the lower spine.                                        |
| press-over   | Overhead pressing makes people arch the lower back.                                       |
| pull-horiz   | Bent-over rows hold the spine under load in a bent position.                              |
| lunge        | Long steps with a heavy load can pull the lower back into extension.                      |

### Stage-by-stage rehab

**Protect.** Goal: keep moving gently and calm the flare-up.
Typical minimum time: 3 days (general practice). Guideline excerpts support staying active and using exercise even in the acute phase (JOSPT 2021 excerpt, grade C for acute with no leg pain, grade B with leg pain).

### Progression criteria notes

- Move up when: minimum days passed, last 3 check-ins 4 or 5, at least 75% of rehab done.
- Also look for: normal walking, sitting and getting out of bed without pain, and no leg symptoms.
- Pain that eases or moves up out of the leg towards the back is a good sign (general practice, unverified).
- Drop back when: any check-in of 2 or lower, 2 skipped rehab sessions in a row, or new leg symptoms (pain, tingling or numbness).
- Keep expectations realistic. Most strains improve over weeks, and a bit of ache while moving is not a reason to stop (NHS excerpt, JOSPT 2021 excerpt).

### Red flags specific to this region

- Numbness or pins and needles in the saddle area (inner thighs, genitals, back passage, buttocks), or changed feeling when wiping. Action: emergency care now (call emergency services or go to A&E). Do not drive yourself.
- New problems passing urine or stool (difficulty starting, leaking, loss of control, not feeling it). Action: emergency care now.
- New weakness in a leg or foot, or numbness in both legs. Action: emergency care now.
- Back pain with fever, unexplained weight loss, or a history of cancer. Action: see a doctor promptly.
- Back pain after a fall, a car crash or other impact, especially with osteoporosis or steroid use. Action: stop loading and get medical care.
- Constant pain that does not change with position or rest, or severe night pain. Action: stop loading the area and see a doctor.

## Sources (combined)

### Low back (L)

- L1: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34719942/ and https://www.orthopt.org/uploads/content_files/files/jospt.2021.0304.pdf — JOSPT 2021 low back pain guideline revision. Supported: exercise is recommended for acute (grade C, no leg pain; grade B with leg pain) and chronic (grade A) pain, including trunk strength and endurance. Excerpt only.
- L2: https://www.nhs.uk/nhs-services/get-nhs-help-for-back-joint-problems/check-if-the-service-is-right-for-you/ — NHS. Supported: bladder, bowel, saddle numbness or sexual changes with back or leg pain need emergency care (call 999 or A&E). Excerpt only.
- L3: https://www.kch.nhs.uk/wp-content/uploads/2024/09/4139-Cauda-equina-syndrome-patient-Info-2024-v6_FINAL.pdf — NHS trust leaflet. Supported: the cauda equina warning-sign list (saddle numbness, bladder and bowel change, leg weakness) and urgency. Excerpt only.
- L4: https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/back-pain/ — NHS back pain page. Could not be fetched. Statements that most strains improve over weeks and that staying active helps are "general practice, unverified" in this file apart from the L1 grading.

### Hamstring (M)

- M1: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20118524/ — Heiderscheit 2010, JOSPT. Supported: neuromuscular and eccentric training lower reinjury risk, about a third of strains recur within a year. Excerpt only.
- M2: https://www.orthopt.org/uploads/content_files/files/Hamstring_Strain_Injury_in_Athletes.pdf — JOSPT 2022 hamstring CPG (PDF excerpt). Supported: use injury history in return decisions, eccentric training, time to return is predicted by pain, days to pain-free walking, strength and tenderness area. Excerpt only.
- M3: https://zaguan.unizar.es/record/162452?ln=fr — Systematic review and meta-analysis of hamstring rehab protocols. Supported: lengthening (eccentric) protocols gave fewer reinjuries and faster return. Excerpt only.
- M4: https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9916392/ — Hu et al 2023, meta-analysis of hamstring eccentric training for injury prevention. Supported: eccentric training reduces hamstring injury rate; compliance matters. Excerpt only.
- M5: https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10053564/ — Systematic review of conservative treatment for proximal hamstring tendinopathy. Supported: staged loading, lumbopelvic work, no strong trials; extrapolated from other tendons. Excerpt only.
- M6: https://ijspt.scholasticahq.com/article/138308-treatment-of-proximal-hamstring-tendinopathy-with-individualized-physiotherapy-a-clinical-commentary — IJSPT clinical commentary. Supported: multi-stage progressive loading, compression at the sit bone as a factor, slow recovery (months). Excerpt only. The "avoid deep stretching" advice comes from clinic pages (https://www.pogophysio.com.au/blog/proximal-hamstring-tendinopathy-exercise/) and is expert opinion.

### Groin (G)

- G1: https://www.aafp.org/afp/1999/0415/p2349 — summary of Hölmich 1999 (Lancet) trial. Supported: active adductor and core strengthening beat passive treatment for longstanding adductor pain. Excerpt only.
- G2: https://www.physio-network.com/research-reviews/hip-groin/return-to-sport-after-criteria-based-rehabilitation-of-acute-adductor-injuries-in-male-athletes-a-prospective-cohort-study/ — Serner et al cohort summary. Supported: criteria-based rehab; meeting pain-free criteria linked to fewer reinjuries. Excerpt only.
- G3: https://www.physiotutors.com/vi/groin-pain-adductor-injuries/ — physiotherapy education site. Supported: Doha groin pain categories, an illustrative phase outline, isometric start and Copenhagen hold example. Clinic resource, not peer-reviewed.
- G4: https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT05970003 — Copenhagen vs Hölmich trial record. Supported: Copenhagen exercise is a high-intensity exercise needing no special equipment, with a side-lying progression. Registry entry, no results used.

### Hip (H)

- H1: https://bmcmusculoskeletdisord.biomedcentral.com/track/pdf/10.1186/s12891-016-1043-6 — LEAP trial protocol (education plus exercise for outer hip tendon pain). Supported: 8-week programme with load advice. Excerpt only.
- H2: https://www.physiotutors.com/conditions/greater-trochanteric-pain-syndrome — physiotherapy education site. Supported: outer hip pain presentation, avoiding compression positions, isometric start. Clinic resource.
- H3: https://my.clevelandclinic.org/health/diseases/21158-femoroacetabular-impingement-fai — Cleveland Clinic. Supported: FAI as a cause of deep groin and front hip pain (the search excerpt covered diagnosis only; details on symptoms are from other pages, marked general practice).
- H4: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36150666/ — narrative review of labral tears and hip pain. Supported: labral problems as a source of hip pain.
- H5: https://www.rcemlearning.org/?p=184889 — emergency medicine learning page on patients who cannot bear weight. Supported: inability to bear weight after a fall is treated as possibly urgent. Excerpt only. https://ufhealth.org/care-sheets/greater-trochanteric-pain-syndrome supports outer hip pain causes (excerpt only).
